What credit costs here

Liqwid works like an on-chain savings and loans desk: depositors lend into a pool and earn interest, and borrowers pay it, posting collateral first. Collateral is what someone locks up to back what they owe or have minted; if its value stops covering that, it goes to whoever pays off the debt. Liqwid, the largest lending market on Cardano, holds 18.21 million dollars of user deposits against 10.76 million dollars of loans across 34 markets. ADA deposits there earn 3.1 percent, with only 42 percent of the pool borrowed. DJED, a stablecoin built to stay worth one dollar, earns its depositors 22.4 percent, with 90 percent borrowed. Only 344.1 thousand dollars of that pool is not already lent out. The rate is that high because the pool is nearly empty of spare dollars, which is also why so little can earn it. The demand on this venue is for dollars, not for ADA, and the gap between those two numbers is how strongly. Why it matters: the rates tell you what people on Cardano actually want to borrow, which no price chart shows.

Who is building: Mithril

One more thing. Who is building Cardano. Today: Mithril, and the team behind it, Teragone Factory. When a new computer joins Cardano, it normally has to download and check the network's entire history, which Mithril's own documentation puts at over a day. Mithril is a shortcut. Stake pools, the independent operators who run Cardano's network, vouch for a snapshot of the chain together, so a newcomer can start from that snapshot in under twenty minutes. Right now about 210 stake pools take part. Recently finished: making the snapshots faster to check. Next on their list: sharing the snapshots over a peer-to-peer network, so no single server has to host them. Last quarter they finished everything on their public plan. Why it matters: the easier it is to run your own copy of Cardano, the more people can check the network for themselves instead of trusting someone else's.
